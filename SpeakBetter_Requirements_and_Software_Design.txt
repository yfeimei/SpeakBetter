SpeakBetter — English Speaking Coach
Requirements and Software Design Document

Revision 4 — paragraphs for Intermediate and Advanced

    Intermediate and Advanced practice content becomes paragraphs of two
    to four sentences rather than single sentences. Reading connected
    speech is a different skill: intonation has to carry across a
    sentence boundary, and breaths have to land in sensible places.

    This forced three consequential changes, recorded in sections 5 and
    8: the recording budget now scales with the length of the passage,
    speech recognition runs in continuous mode so it does not stop at
    the first full stop, and scoring is normalized by passage length
    instead of using fixed penalties.

    Sections affected: 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 9, 10, 12 (FR-14), 16, 18, 22, 23.

Revision 3 — self-playback, custom sentences, suggestions

    Adds three capabilities: the learner can play back their own
    recording next to the model pronunciation, practice a sentence they
    type themselves, and receive improvement suggestions even when the
    reading was correct.

    Sections affected: 6, 7, 12 (FR-13 to FR-15), 16, 18, 20, 21, 22, 23.

Revision 2 — client-side architecture

    This revision replaces the FastAPI backend of revision 1 with a
    fully client-side design. Speech recognition now runs in the browser,
    which removed the only part of the system that genuinely required a
    server. Section 17 records the reasoning behind that change.

    Sections affected: 12 (FR-6), 13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18, 20, 21, 22.

============================================================
1. PROJECT OVERVIEW
============================================================

Project Name:
SpeakBetter — English Speaking Coach

Purpose:
SpeakBetter is a web application that helps English learners practice
speaking. The user reads a sentence, listens to the correct pronunciation,
records themselves speaking, receives automated feedback, and tries again.

The MVP does not require an LLM, a server, or a user account.

Core learning loop:

    Choose Practice
          ↓
    See a sentence
          ↓
    Listen
          ↓
    Record
          ↓
    Analyze
          ↓
    See score and feedback
          ↓
    Try Again
          ↓
    Next sentence

2. PRODUCT REQUIREMENTS

2.1 Main User Experience

The application should feel like a simple personal speaking coach.

The user should be able to:

1. Select a difficulty level.
2. See an English practice sentence.
3. Listen to the correct pronunciation.
4. Record their own speech.
5. Submit the recording.
6. See a speaking-match score.
7. See words that were missed, added, or significantly different.
8. Receive simple coaching tips.
9. Listen to the correct version again.
10. Try the sentence again.
11. See basic practice progress.

============================================================
5. RECORDING
============================================================

While recording, the page shows:

    Recording...

           ●            ← pulses with the user's voice level

         00:04

    ▓▓▓▓▓▓░░░░░░░░░░    ← elapsed / remaining

    "i would like a cup"   ← live partial transcript

    [ ■ Stop Recording ]

The browser requests microphone permission and uses the MediaRecorder
API. The pulsing dot is driven by a Web Audio AnalyserNode measuring the
live input level, so the user can see that they are being heard.


5.1 Recording budget

The time limit scales with the length of the passage, because a
paragraph cannot be read inside a sentence-sized window:

    budget = 4 s + 0.7 s per word,
             floored at 15 s, capped at 60 s

    "I like coffee."             3 words   -> 15 s (floor)
    An intermediate paragraph   21 words   -> 19 s
    An advanced paragraph       39 words   -> 31 s

The button shows the budget before recording starts, and the progress
bar drains against it.


5.2 Continuous recognition

For anything longer than twelve words, speech recognition runs in
continuous mode.

This is not optional. With continuous mode off, the recognizer treats
the first pause as the end of the utterance — so on a three-sentence
paragraph it would transcribe the opening sentence, stop, and report
everything after the first full stop as missing. The learner would be
told they had skipped two-thirds of a passage they read perfectly well.

Single sentences keep continuous mode off, which preserves the pleasant
behaviour of stopping automatically as soon as the speaker finishes.
Paragraphs stop on the Stop button or the budget above.

6. ANALYSIS RESULT
============================================================

Example target sentence:

    "I think this is a good idea."

Example user speech:

    "I tink this is good idea."

Actual result produced by the implementation:

    Your Result

             71 / 100
       SPEAKING MATCH · KEEP PRACTICING
          ▲ 12 from last try

    I    think    this    is    a    good    idea
    ✓      ⚠       ✓      ✓    ⚠      ✓       ✓

    We heard: "I tink this is good idea"

    • You missed "a".
    • Your speech was slightly different around "think" —
      it sounded like "tink".

    PRONUNCIATION TIP
    "think" — TH sound
    Put your tongue lightly between your teeth and let air flow
    through. Do not say "tink" or "sink".

    WHAT TO WORK ON NEXT
    Speak up a little
    The recognizer was unsure of some words even where they matched.
    Try moving closer to the microphone and opening your mouth a
    little wider.

    Keep an ear on "this"
    You got it this time. Voiced TH. Your throat vibrates. "This"
    should not sound like "dis".

    COMPARE
    Hear the model pronunciation, then your own attempt straight after.

    CORRECT   [ 🔊 Listen ]
    YOU       [ ▶ ————•——————  0:04 ]

    [ ↺ Try Again ]      [ Next Sentence → ]


Two errors — one mispronounced word and one missing word — across seven
words: 1 - 2/7 gives 71.

A tip appears only when the problem word matches a coaching rule. In
this example "think" does; the missing "a" does not, and correctly
produces no tip.

Two elements are always present, whatever the score:

    Compare       The model pronunciation and the learner's own
                  recording, side by side. Hearing the difference is
                  often more useful than reading about it.

    What to work  Suggestions from the suggestion engine (section
    on next       20.4). These appear even on a perfect reading, so
                  there is always a next thing to try.


The score is presented as a speaking-match score, not as a
scientifically validated pronunciation score.

Word marks:

    ✓   correct    spoken as written
    ⚠   problem    recognized as a similar but different word
    ⚠   missing    in the sentence but never spoken
    +   extra      spoken but not in the sentence

When the same sentence is attempted more than once, the change from the
previous attempt is shown beneath the score. This is what makes
improvement visible during a demonstration.


============================================================
7. PRONUNCIATION COACHING
============================================================

The MVP provides predefined pronunciation tips.

Example:

    "think"

    Pronunciation Tip

    Focus on the TH sound.

    Put your tongue lightly between your teeth and let air flow through.

    [ Listen ]

    [ Try Again ]


The coaching rules are stored locally and do not require an LLM.

Example rules:

    think → TH sound
    three → TH sound
    right → R sound
    very  → V sound

At most three tips are shown at once, one per distinct sound, so the
result stays readable.


============================================================
8. SCORING
============================================================

A speaking-match score from 0 to 100: the share of the passage that came
through correctly.

    score = 100 x (1 - weighted errors / words in the target)

    Missing word      1.0
    Mismatched word   1.0
    Extra word        0.5

    Clamped to the range 0-100.

An extra word weighs less than a missing one. Adding a word still shows
the learner produced the target text; dropping one means part of it was
never said at all.

The error counts come from the word-level alignment described in section
19, so each one corresponds to exactly one alignment operation.


8.1 Why not fixed penalties

Revision 1 specified a fixed scheme: start at 100, subtract 10 per
missing or mismatched word and 5 per extra word. That works for single
sentences and fails badly for paragraphs.

    A forty-word paragraph read with ten slips scores zero under fixed
    penalties. The learner got three-quarters of it right. Zero tells
    them nothing, and is simply not true.

Fixed penalties also make the same mistake cost the same regardless of
how much was being read, so a slip in a three-word sentence and a slip
in a fifty-word paragraph are treated as equally serious. They are not.

Normalizing fixes both, and gives the number a meaning a learner can
hold on to: roughly the percentage of the passage they said correctly.
Scores become comparable across levels.

This is the normalized similarity score that revision 1 already listed
as the acceptable alternative, so it is a change of default rather than
a departure from the original design.


8.2 Naming

The score is described as:

    Speaking Match

rather than claiming it is a scientifically validated pronunciation
score. It measures how closely the recognizer's transcript matches the
target text, which is a useful practice signal but not an assessment of
pronunciation quality.


============================================================
9. PRACTICE CONTENT
============================================================

30 items, 10 at each level, chosen to exercise the sounds the coaching
engine knows about (TH, R, V, W, L) and to grow in length with the level.

Beginner is single sentences. Intermediate and Advanced are paragraphs,
because reading connected speech — carrying intonation across a sentence
boundary, breathing in sensible places, not resetting to a flat pitch
after every full stop — is a different skill from reading one sentence
in isolation.

    Level          Form                    Typical length

    Beginner       one sentence            4-7 words
    Intermediate   two or three sentences  20-25 words
    Advanced       three or four sentences 30-40 words

Beginner examples:

    "I like coffee."
    "My name is John."
    "I live in Chicago."

Intermediate example:

    "I usually go to work by train. The journey takes about forty
     minutes, so I read a book on the way."

Advanced example:

    "I have been working on this project for several months. Although
     the results are encouraging, there is still a great deal to do
     before we can publish anything. I would rather take the extra
     time and get it right."

============================================================
12. FUNCTIONAL REQUIREMENTS
============================================================

FR-1 — Select Exercise
The user shall be able to select a difficulty level.

FR-2 — Display Exercise
The system shall display an English sentence.

FR-3 — Play Correct Pronunciation
The user shall be able to hear the target sentence using text-to-speech,
at normal or reduced speed.

FR-4 — Record Speech
The browser shall record the user's microphone input.

FR-5 — Submit Recording
The recording shall be submitted for analysis when the user stops
recording.

FR-6 — Speech-to-Text
The browser shall transcribe the recording using the Web Speech
Recognition API. No audio is transmitted by the application.

FR-7 — Compare Speech
The system shall compare recognized text against the target sentence.

FR-8 — Generate Score
The system shall calculate a speaking-match score.

FR-9 — Identify Problems
The system shall identify missing, extra, and significantly different
words.

FR-10 — Provide Coaching
The system shall display predefined coaching information for supported
pronunciation problems.

FR-11 — Retry
The user shall be able to record the sentence again.

FR-12 — Progress
The system shall maintain basic practice history.

FR-13 — Replay Own Recording
The user shall be able to play back their own recording alongside the
model pronunciation.

FR-14 — Custom Sentence or Paragraph
The user shall be able to enter text of their own, up to 400 characters,
and practice it with the same analysis, scoring, and coaching as a
library item. Recently used entries shall be offered for reuse.

The entry point shall be reachable from the home page, from the practice
page before recording, and from the result card afterwards — wanting to
practice your own words is as likely after seeing a score as before.

FR-15 — Improvement Suggestions
The system shall offer at least one suggestion for what to work on next
after every attempt, including attempts that scored perfectly.


============================================================
13. NON-FUNCTIONAL REQUIREMENTS
============================================================

Performance:
Analysis is local text processing and completes in well under a second
once the transcript is available. The perceived wait is the browser's
recognition step, typically one to two seconds after the user stops
speaking. There is no server to wake and no cold start.

Usability:
The user should understand what to do without extensive instructions.

Privacy:
Recorded audio is never uploaded by the application. It is held in
browser memory as a Blob, used only for the timer, the level meter, and
optional self-playback, and released with URL.revokeObjectURL as soon as
the user retries or moves on.

Actual flow:

    Record
      ↓
    Transcribe in browser
      ↓
    Analyze in browser
      ↓
    Discard audio

One caveat is stated openly in the README: Chrome's implementation of
the Web Speech Recognition API sends audio to Google's speech service to
transcribe it. That is browser behaviour rather than application
behaviour, but "runs in your browser" does not mean "audio never leaves
your device."

Reliability:
The application handles, each with a specific message:

- microphone permission denied
- no microphone present
- empty recording
- speech not recognized
- network unavailable
- speech service failure
- browser without speech recognition support

If recognition neither completes nor reports an error, the application
settles after a five-second grace period rather than hanging.

Storage failures are non-fatal. In private browsing the application
still works; it simply does not remember progress.

============================================================
17. ARCHITECTURE DECISION: NO BACKEND
============================================================

Revision 1 of this document specified a FastAPI backend containing a
Practice API, a Speech Analysis Service, a Text Comparison Engine, and a
Coaching Engine. That backend has been removed. This section records why.

17.1 What the backend actually did

    Practice API            Served a static JSON file.
                            Needs no server.

    Text Comparison Engine  Normalize, tokenize, align, score.
                            Pure text processing. Needs no server.

    Coaching Engine         A dictionary lookup.
                            Needs no server.

    Speech-to-Text          The one component that could not run
                            as client-side application logic.

Three of the four components were already server-independent. The entire
backend existed to serve one endpoint, and the only part of that endpoint
requiring a server was transcription.

17.2 Why transcription no longer requires one

The Web Speech Recognition API performs recognition in the browser at no
cost and with no key. Once transcription moves to the client, nothing is
left for a server to do.

17.3 Consequences

Gained:

    No cold starts. Free backend tiers sleep after inactivity and take
    30-60 seconds to wake. A reviewer opening the public URL for the
    first time would have hit that delay on their first recording,
    directly conflicting with the responsiveness goal in section 13.

    No API key. A speech API key cannot be shipped safely in a static
    frontend; it would be readable by anyone and billable to us. Holding
    that key was the strongest remaining argument for a server, and it
    disappears with browser recognition.

    Stronger privacy. Audio is never uploaded by the application.

    Free hosting, and no operational burden.

    Simpler deployment: one artifact instead of two.

Lost:

    Browser support. Recognition works in Chrome and Edge, partially in
    Safari, and not in Firefox. The application detects this on load,
    explains it, and leaves the sentences and Listen button working.

    Server-side accuracy. A dedicated speech API would transcribe more
    accurately than the browser, particularly for strong accents.

    An internet connection is still required, because browser
    recognition is itself network-backed.

17.4 Keeping the option open

`analyzeSpeech()` in `services/api.ts` preserves the request and response
shape of the original `POST /api/analyze` endpoint and is the only place
in the application that knows where analysis happens. Reintroducing a
server later means changing that one function; no component, page, or
engine module would need to change.

This is the reason the engine layer is kept free of browser APIs: those
same three modules would port to a Python or Node service directly.


============================================================
18. MODULE INTERFACES
============================================================

The HTTP API of revision 1 is now a module interface. The data shapes are
unchanged, which is what keeps section 17.4 possible.

Get exercises — replaces `GET /api/exercises?level=beginner`:

    fetchExercises(level: Level): Exercise[]

Example element:

    {
      "id": 12,
      "text": "I would like to order a cup of coffee.",
      "level": "intermediate"
    }


Analyze speech — replaces `POST /api/analyze`:

    analyzeSpeech(
      exerciseId: number,
      recognized: string,
      meta?: SpeechMeta,
    ): AnalysisResult

    Throws AnalysisError for an unknown exercise or an empty transcript.

Analyze any text — used for learner-supplied sentences:

    analyzeText(
      expected: string,
      recognized: string,
      meta?: SpeechMeta,
    ): AnalysisResult

    analyzeSpeech looks the sentence up by id and delegates here, so
    both paths score identically.

Validate a learner-supplied sentence:

    validateCustomSentence(input: string):
      { ok: true, text: string } | { ok: false, error: string }

    Trims and collapses whitespace. Rejects an empty sentence, one with
    no letters, or one longer than 200 characters.

Optional recording signals:

    interface SpeechMeta {
      durationMs?: number   // how long the learner recorded
      confidence?: number   // recognizer confidence, 0 to 1
    }

    Both are optional because the browser does not always report them.
    A confidence of 0 means "not reported" and is ignored rather than
    treated as a poor result.

The examples below are verbatim output from the implementation.

Example result — analyzeSpeech(1, "I like coffee"):

      "recognized": "I like coffee",

============================================================
19. TEXT COMPARISON ENGINE
============================================================

19.1 Normalization

Both the target sentence and the transcript pass through an identical
pipeline, so any difference that survives is a real speaking difference
rather than an artifact of transcription style.

    lowercase
    fold curly quotes and unicode dashes
    expand contractions       "I'd"  → "i would"
    split hyphens             "well-known" → "well known"
    strip punctuation
    spell out digits          "3"    → "three"

Example:

    "I think, this is a good idea!"

becomes:

    "i think this is a good idea"

Tokenize:

    ["i", "think", "this", "is", "a", "good", "idea"]

Contraction expansion and digit spelling matter more than they look.
A recognizer may return "I would" where the learner said "I'd", or "3"
where the sentence reads "three". Without normalization each of those
would be reported as a mistake the learner did not make.

19.2 Alignment

Word-level Levenshtein distance with a backtrace. The dynamic
programming table is filled with these costs, then walked backwards to
recover the individual operations:

    delete   (missing word)      1.0
    insert   (extra word)        1.0
    substitute, similar words    1.0
    substitute, different words  1.6

Costs are stored as integers scaled by ten, so the backtrace can compare
table entries with exact equality rather than a floating-point tolerance.

Two words count as similar when their character-level edit distance
gives a similarity of 0.5 or more:

    similarity(a, b) = 1 - editDistance(a, b) / max(len(a), len(b))

Both substitution costs stay below delete + insert (2.0), so a near-miss
aligns as one imperfect word rather than a dropped word plus an added
one. This is what makes "think" heard as "tink" read as a single
mispronunciation:

    Expected:
    I think this is a good idea

    Actual:
    I tink this is a good idea

    Result:

    i       ✓
    think   ⚠   heard as "tink"
    this    ✓
    is      ✓
    a       ✓
    good    ✓
    idea    ✓

    Score: 90

The alignment is order-preserving: reading the operations left to right
reconstructs both sequences exactly, which is asserted by a unit test.

19.3 Scoring

Each alignment operation maps to one penalty, as listed in section 8.

20. COACHING AND SUGGESTION ENGINES
============================================================

Rules live in `data/pronunciationRules.ts`. The engine performs a lookup
in two layers, checked in order.

20.1 Word rules

A tip written for one specific word. Around 38 entries covering TH, R,
V, W, L, SH, ZH and multi-syllable words.

    {
      "think": {
        "sound": "TH",
        "tip": "Put your tongue lightly between your teeth and let air
                flow through."
      },
      "three": {
        "sound": "TH",
        "tip": "Put your tongue lightly between your teeth, then add
                the R."
      },
      "right": {
        "sound": "R",
        "tip": "Raise your tongue without touching the roof of your
                mouth."
      }
    }

20.2 Sound rules

Patterns matched against any word with no hand-written entry, so
coverage is not limited to the list above.

    TH   /th/
    R    /^r|rr/
    V    /v/
    W    /^w/
    SH   /tion$|sh/
    L    /^l|ll/
    ED   /ed$/
    S    /s$/

20.3 Selection

Tips are collected for words marked `problem` or `missing`, one per
distinct sound, to a maximum of three.


20.4 Suggestion engine

The coaching engine explains what went wrong. The suggestion engine
answers a different question: what should the learner work on next?

It runs on every attempt, including a perfect one, so a score of 100
still comes with something to try. Suggestions are built only from
signals that were actually measured — never from a guess about
pronunciation quality.

    Signal                Source                    Suggestion

    Recognizer            SpeechRecognition          clarity
    confidence            result confidence

    Speaking pace         recording duration and     pace
                          recognized word count

    Rule-backed word      the sentence text and      sound
    spoken correctly      the coaching rules

    Long sentence         word count and score       fluency
    read well

    High score            score                      challenge

At most three are shown, and at least one always is.


20.5 Measuring pace honestly

Pace is reported in words per minute, computed from the recording
duration. Two corrections keep it from giving bad advice:

    Overhead     A recording includes the moment between clicking
                 Record and starting to speak, plus the silence the
                 recognizer waits through before deciding the sentence
                 has ended — roughly a second in Chrome. A fixed
                 1200 ms allowance is subtracted before the rate is
                 computed.

    Short        Below six words the overhead dominates whatever is
    sentences    left, and the number says more about reaction time
                 than about speaking. Short sentences get no pace
                 advice at all.

Thresholds are deliberately wide — below 75 wpm or above 190 wpm — so
only clear extremes are remarked on. Anything in between passes without
comment rather than inventing a criticism.

Without these two corrections, "I like coffee" spoken normally in three
seconds computes as 60 wpm and would be wrongly flagged as too slow.


No LLM is required for any of this.

    The application listens to a human being,

    identifies a problem,
    provides feedback,
    and measures improvement.

simply a webpage that displays an AI response.


============================================================
25. DESIGN PRINCIPLE
============================================================

Keep the project small enough to finish in two days.

The objective is NOT to build a production-level pronunciation
assessment system.

The objective is to build a polished, working demonstration that
combines:

    Browser audio recording
        +
    Speech recognition
        +
    Text analysis
        +
    Automated feedback
        +
    Text-to-speech
        +
    Interactive retry
        +
    Progress tracking

The application should be something a college admissions reviewer can
open through a public URL and try immediately.

Removing the backend serves this principle directly. It deleted an
entire deployment target, a runtime cost, a secret to manage, and a
cold-start delay on the reviewer's first click — while removing nothing
the user can see.
